# Research: candidate texts for *Touch is not one sense*

Companion to [HANDOFF.md](HANDOFF.md). Where the handoff records what was
decided, this records **what else is out there** — texts checked against source
during a research pass on 2026-09-06, assessed for whether they belong in this
story and whether they could carry an interactive figure.

Nothing here is built. Each entry is written so that a decision can be made
without re-reading the paper.

**How each entry is written**, following the editorial standard in HANDOFF §3:

- the citation, in the form it would take in `refs.bib`
- **What it found** — verified numbers, from the paper or its abstract
- **Weight** — the honest one-sentence assessment, written *before* any prose
- **Where it would sit** and what it would displace
- **The figure**, and what the interaction teaches that prose cannot
- **What could go wrong** — the error class this one invites

Everything marked *verified* was read from the paper, its publisher abstract, or
a metadata record. Section 6 lists what could **not** be verified and must be
read in the original before it is used. Do not promote anything out of section 6
without opening the paper.

---

## 1. What the page is actually missing

Three gaps, in order of how much they cost:

**The newest reference is 2023.** Two things have landed since that bear
directly on stops the page already has — a 137-study meta-analysis of touch
interventions (2024) and the first human evidence that CT afferents are hair
follicle receptors (2025). A page whose whole argument is *check what the
evidence actually says* is weakened by being two years behind it.

**The closing figure has no study behind it.** ~~*Meaning* is the strongest note
the page has and the only figure labelled illustrative.~~ **Closed 2026-09-06.**
The figure now has two halves either side of a divider: the verdict words above
it, still illustrative and still labelled so, and Gazzola's measured ratings
below it. See §2.4.

**Nothing in the page is about the practitioner's own hands.** Every figure
describes what happens in the person being touched. The reader is the one
touching, and the two facts most specific to them — that a hand cannot produce
on itself what it produces on someone else, and that their own fingertips carry
four times the receptor density of their palm — are both absent.

## 2. Tier one — the four worth building

### 2.1 Packheiser et al. 2024 — the meta-analysis the page predates

> Packheiser J, Hartmann H, Fredriksen K, Gazzola V, Keysers C, Michon F (2024).
> "A systematic review and multivariate meta-analysis of the physical and mental
> health benefits of touch interventions." *Nature Human Behaviour* 8:1088–1107.
> https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01841-8

**What it found** *(verified)*. 137 studies in the meta-analysis, 75 more in the
review, 12,966 people. Adults: 85 studies, Hedges' *g* = 0.52 (95% CI
0.42–0.63). Newborns: 52 studies, *g* = 0.56 (0.41–0.71). Mental and physical
outcomes did not differ from each other in either group. Pain reduction
*g* = 0.69 (0.48–0.89). Cortisol in adults *g* = 0.78 (0.24–1.31). Moderators,
which are the interesting part:

| Question | Answer | Number |
|---|---|---|
| Does it matter *who* touches an adult? | No detectable difference | *g* diff 0.02 (−0.27 to 0.24), familiar vs unfamiliar |
| Does it matter who touches a **newborn**? | Yes — parents beat staff | *g* diff 0.30 (0.01–0.59) |
| Does a **longer session** help? | No, and some outcomes went the other way | no positive association |
| Do **more sessions** help? | Yes, steadily | *g* +0.02 per session (0.01–0.03) |
| Human hand or object/robot? | Same for physical, human better for mental | 0.56 vs 0.51; 0.58 vs 0.34 |
| Where on the body? | Head beat arm and torso | *g* diff 0.78–0.84 |

**Weight.** The largest and most recent synthesis in the field, and it says so
itself that it is built on a literature with a small-study bias it can measure
(adults *F*(1,101) = 21.24, *p* < 0.001) and blinding that is impossible by the
nature of the intervention. Strong for direction and ordering, soft for the size
of any single number.

**Where it would sit.** *How sure are we* (stop eleven), and it changes that stop
materially. `CLAIMS` currently places seven claims on a 0–1 line by judgement,
and the caption says so. Half of them now have a measured effect size and a
confidence interval instead.

**The figure.** Keep the predict-then-reveal — it is the right pattern and the
page's own note says it is the only retrieval practice present. But reveal a
**measured interval**, not a hand-placed dot: the reader's marker lands beside a
real 95% CI. Then a second panel the prose genuinely cannot do — four toggles
for *who*, *how long*, *how many*, *where*, each moving a single effect-size bar.
The lesson is in the pattern the toggles make: **frequency moves it, duration
does not**, which is the opposite of how most treatment plans are sold.

**What could go wrong.** This is the error class HANDOFF §5 names — a correct
measurement carrying a conclusion it cannot support. "Head touch is best,
*g* = 0.8" would be exactly that: head touch appears in a different literature
(scalp massage, comforting a child) than torso touch, and the moderator cannot
separate the site from the setting. If the figure shows the body-part bar it has
to say that beside it. Same for "more sessions": the association is across
studies, not a dose given to one person.

**A tension to keep, not resolve.** No difference between a familiar and an
unfamiliar toucher in adults sits directly against Coan 2006 and Goldstein 2018,
both already on the page, and against `CLAIMS` entry *"Who is touching you
changes how much it helps"* at 0.80. Both can be true — a partner's hand beats a
stranger's *for threat response in an fMRI scanner*, while across 85 heterogenous
studies of health outcomes the toucher's identity washes out. Part four is where
that belongs, stated as the disagreement it is. Do not quietly lower the 0.80.

### 2.2 Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert 1999 — why your hand cannot do this to itself

> Blakemore S-J, Frith CD, Wolpert DM (1999). "Spatio-temporal prediction
> modulates the perception of self-produced stimuli." *Journal of Cognitive
> Neuroscience* 11(5):551–559. https://doi.org/10.1162/089892999563607
>
> Companion imaging study: Blakemore S-J, Wolpert DM, Frith CD (1998). "Central
> cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation." *Nature Neuroscience*
> 1(7):635–640. Review: same authors (2000), *NeuroReport* 11(11):R11–R16.

**What it found** *(verified from the 2000 review, which reports the experiment
in full).* 16 people. A piece of soft foam on a robot arm moved across the right
palm — sinusoidal, amplitude 1.5 cm, 2 Hz, **identical in every condition**. The
left hand drove the robot. The only thing that changed was the correspondence
between the movement and its consequence: delays of 0, 100, 200 and 300 ms, and
in a separate condition trajectory rotations of 0°, 30°, 60° and 90°.

Self-produced touch was rated significantly less tickly, less intense and less
pleasant than the same stimulus produced by the robot (*p* < 0.001). Tickliness
then rose **progressively** with delay from 0 to 200 ms (*p* < 0.0005) and with
rotation from 0° to 90° (*p* < 0.01). In the 1998 imaging study, somatosensory
cortex and anterior cingulate responded less to self-produced touch than to the
same touch produced by the experimenter.

**Weight.** Small samples, thirty years old, and replicated many times since;
the phenomenon is not in doubt and every reader can check the endpoint on their
own palm in about four seconds. The forward-model account of *why* is a model,
and should be described as one.

**Where it would sit.** HANDOFF §7 already calls this "the best single omission
to fix." It is a stop of its own, in **part three**, after *pain* — a third thing
touch does. It must come before *meaning*, per HANDOFF §9.

**The figure.** Two sliders, delay and rotation, over a hand and a moving contact
point; a ticklishness reading that climbs as either slider moves off zero. The
interaction carries the whole claim, which prose states badly and a static chart
states twice: **the attenuation is not caused by moving — it is caused by
predicting correctly.** A reader who drags delay from 0 to 300 ms and watches a
sensation appear out of nothing has understood the forward model without the
term being defined.

This is also the page's most defensible interaction, because the underlying
result *is* a two-parameter response surface. Everything else here has to be
given an interaction; this one arrives with it.

**Why it matters to this reader specifically.** It is the mechanism behind
something every practitioner has noticed and probably explains wrongly: a client
cannot give themselves what the practitioner gives them. Not because the
practitioner's hand is special, but because the client's own hand is predicted.
That is a genuinely useful, genuinely established thing to be able to say, and it
belongs in *what we would carry back into the room*.

**What could go wrong.** The slide from "self-touch is attenuated" to "so
self-massage does not work" — false, and the same species of error as the
two-point sentence in HANDOFF §5. The finding is about the *tickle and intensity
of light moving contact*, measured on the palm. It says nothing about pressure,
tissue, or whether self-applied work helps anyone.

### 2.3 Johansson & Vallbo 1979 — the number under a square centimetre

> Johansson RS, Vallbo ÅB (1979). "Tactile sensibility in the human hand:
> relative and absolute densities of four types of mechanoreceptive units in
> glabrous skin." *Journal of Physiology* 286:283–300.
> https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1979.sp012619

**What it found** *(verified from the abstract).* 334 low-threshold
mechanoreceptive units recorded from the median nerve in awake people, sorted
into the four types the page already draws (RA, SA I, SA II, PC). Density rises
from palm to fingertip: relative densities **1 : 1.6 : 4.2** for palm : finger
body : fingertip, and in absolute terms about **241 units/cm² at the fingertip
against 58 units/cm² in the palm**. The rise is carried by RA and SA I — the two
with small receptive fields. PC and SA II are spread far more evenly.

**Weight.** The measurement the whole field is built on, from the same
microneurography laboratory as Vallbo 1999 which the page already cites, and
still quoted unchanged. Solid.

**Where it would sit.** It strengthens two existing stops without needing a new
one — the skin cross-section (stop seven) and acuity (stop three).

**The figure.** The receptor figure currently answers *what is down there*. This
lets it answer *how many, and where*. Drag along a hand from palm to fingertip
and watch the dot field thicken 4.2×, with the split between the four types
changing as you go — RA and SA I crowding in, PC and SA II staying put. It gives
the acuity ranking a cause instead of leaving it as a list of numbers, and it
does it with a count the reader can see rather than a threshold they have to take
on trust.

**What could go wrong.** Two things. It is **glabrous skin of the hand only** —
it says nothing about the forearm or the back, which is where most of this page's
practice content lives, so it cannot be extended into a whole-body density map.
And it must not be run together with the CT story: these are the fast system's
receptors, in the skin type where CT afferents are sparse (Watkins 2021, already
cited). Blue, not clay.

### 2.4 Gazzola et al. 2012 and Ellingsen et al. 2013 — meaning, with the touch held identical

> **BUILT — 2026-09-06.** Both papers are on the page. `refs.bib` carries
> `gazzola12` and `ellingsen13`; the `meaning` stop's `.src` block names them
> and states both weaknesses; the prose carries Gazzola's swing and Ellingsen's
> opposite-direction dissociation; and `sceneContext` has a second panel below a
> divider, drawn by `believePanel()` in `scenes.js` from `BELIEF` in
> `figure-data.js`, with a fourth control for what the reader believes.
>
> Ellingsen stayed **out of the figure** and went into the prose instead. Its
> rating effects are a fifth of a point against Gazzola's five and a half, and
> putting them on one axis would have made the smaller one look like nothing.
> Its real contribution — sensory processing moving in opposite directions for
> pleasure and pain — is a sentence, not a chart.
>
> The assessment below is kept rather than deleted, because the record of what
> reading the papers changed is the part worth having.

> Gazzola V, Spezio ML, Etzel JA, Castelli F, Adolphs R, Keysers C (2012).
> "Primary somatosensory cortex discriminates affective significance in social
> touch." *PNAS* 109(25):E1657–E1666. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1113211109
>
> Ellingsen D-M, Wessberg J, Eikemo M, Liljencrantz J, Endestad T, Olausson H,
> Leknes S (2013). "Placebo improves pleasure and pain through opposite
> modulation of sensory processing." *PNAS* 110:17993–17998.
> https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1305050110

**What Gazzola found** *(verified from the full text, 2026-09-06).* 18
heterosexual white men, mean age 26.2, range 21–31. Each watched a video of who
was supposedly caressing them. **Every caress was delivered by the same female
experimenter, blind to which video was playing.** Believed-male: −2.53 ± 1.38.
Believed-female: 3.05 ± 0.87, on a −5 to +5 scale (*p* < 0.002). Primary
somatosensory cortex — strongest in Brodmann areas 2 and 1, weakest in 3a —
responded more strongly in the believed-female condition, and its signal
correlated with the reported pleasantness across caresses. **That last
correlation is reported at an uncorrected *p* < 0.005**, which is the weakest
link in the chain and has to be said out loud if the page leans on it.

**What Ellingsen found** *(verified from the full text, 2026-09-06 — this entry
was in §6 until then, and three things changed).* 30 people, 28 analysed, 10 of
them women, mean age 25.5. Within-subject crossover over two sessions,
counterbalanced, the experimenter delivering the touch blind to condition. They
watched a six-minute documentary about oxytocin, then self-administered ten
puffs of a saline spray they were told **could** contain it.

The stimuli, which matter more than the summary suggests: brushing on the back
of the left forearm at about **5 cm/s** for 10 s; a heat pad at about
**42.5 °C**; and painful heat set per person to their own 5-out-of-10, a mean of
47.1 °C. Ratings on a −5 to +5 scale, anchored *unpleasant* and *pleasant*.

Placebo improved every rating, *F*(1,24) = 7.2, *p* = 0.01. Taken separately:
stroking 3.1 → 3.3 (*p* = 0.049), warmth 2.5 → 2.8 (*p* = 0.03), pain −2.4 →
−1.9 (*p* = 0.003). In the imaging, sensory regions moved in **opposite
directions**: up for pleasant touch (posterior insula *Z* = 3.96, SII
*Z* = 3.25), down for pain (SI *Z* = −4.29, SII *Z* = −3.56). Reward and
emotion regions — nucleus accumbens, amygdala, ventromedial prefrontal cortex,
periaqueductal grey — moved the same way for both.

**Three corrections to what this document said before.** They are the reason for
reading the paper rather than the summary.

- **The control session had no spray at all** — "identical to the placebo
  session except that there was no nasal spray administration." So this is
  placebo against nothing, not blinded placebo against blinded placebo. The
  participant always knows which session they are in. *Clean crossover* was too
  generous and is withdrawn.
- **SI was named only for the pain decrease.** For pleasant touch the paper
  reports posterior insula and SII, **not** SI. The primary-somatosensory
  finding for pleasant social touch is **Gazzola's**, not Ellingsen's, and the
  two must not be merged — see the figure note below, which said the wrong
  thing.
- **The pleasantness effects are small.** 0.2 of a point on a ten-point scale at
  *p* = 0.049 is not "striking". The striking part is the imaging dissociation,
  and the pain effect (0.5 of a point, *p* = 0.003) is the sturdier half.

**Weight.** Gazzola: small, all-male, all-white, and confounded by design — the
comparison was an attractive woman who "behaved in a warm and friendly manner"
against a man who "behaved in a more distant manner", so belief about sex,
belief about attractiveness and belief about *demeanour* cannot be separated.
The authors say so themselves. The finding that survives that
confound is the one the page needs, and it is the strong one: **the same physical
touch, believed to come from someone else, changed the rating by more than five
points and changed the response in primary sensory cortex.** Ellingsen is a
within-subject crossover against an unblinded no-treatment control, with small
effects on rating and a clear dissociation in the imaging; it is a placebo study,
so it inherits every caveat placebo studies have, and its own authors say the
mechanism is not pinned down.

**Where it would sit.** *Meaning*, stop twelve. This is the fix for the one
figure on the page carrying no evidence.

**The figure.** Keep the existing three switches — consent, trust, warning — and
keep the physical stroke pinned at 3 cm/s and 32°, because that is the point.
What changes is the panel underneath: the verdict words stop being illustrative
and stand next to two measured results. A fourth control, faithful to Gazzola:
change *who the reader believes is touching them* and watch a rating swing from
−2.5 to +3.1 with nothing about the contact altered. Add a small honest marker
showing that S1 — the fast road, the blue one, the "just information" road the
page introduced in stop one — moved too.

**Correction to that last sentence.** It came from reading Ellingsen second-hand.
Ellingsen did **not** find SI moving for pleasant touch — posterior insula and
SII did, and SI moved only for pain, downward. The S1 marker belongs to
**Gazzola alone**, where SI really is the finding and the paper's title says so.
Draw it from Gazzola, label it Gazzola, and if Ellingsen appears in the same
panel its sensory arrow points *down and for pain*, which is a different and
also interesting thing.

**And a tension with the page's own warmth stop.** Ellingsen's "warm touch" was
about **42.5 °C**. The page's warmth figure, from Ackerley 2014, offers 18°, 32°
and 42° and shows 42° rated *worse* than skin temperature. So Ellingsen's warm
stimulus is not the page's optimum — it is the hot one the page already says
people like less, and belief still improved it. Written carelessly ("warm touch
was rated more pleasant") a reader ties it straight to the 32° stop and gets it
backwards. Written carefully it is a better point than the careless version: the
belief effect showed up on a stimulus that was not even at the good temperature.

**Why this is the best-value item here.** The page's spine is *the felt
experience is real; the mechanism attributed to it often is not*. These two
papers say something sharper and in the page's own favour: meaning does not sit
on top of the sensation as an interpretation of it. It reaches down into the
first cortical relay and changes the sensory response itself. The page currently
ends by asserting that; it could end by showing it.

**What could go wrong.** Overreach in one direction — "it's all in the mind,
technique doesn't matter" — which the page's own rule 10 already resists and
should keep resisting. And under-reporting the Gazzola confound. State it in the
`src` block; a page that has a whole part called *being honest about it* cannot
quietly use the tidy version of this study.

## 3. Tier two — strong, but each needs a new stop or a rebuilt figure

### 3.1 Moore et al. 2025 — the CT afferent is a hair follicle receptor

> Moore W, Nikesjö J, Bouchatta O, Makdani AD, Hakizimana P, Rousson M,
> Duvernoy B, McIntyre S, Pehkonen LJ, Fridberger A, McGlone F, Olausson H,
> Nagi SS, Marshall A (2025). "Robust coupling between the C-tactile afferent
> and the hair follicle in humans." *Journal of Physiology* 603(16):4593–4608.
> https://doi.org/10.1113/JP287706
>
> Commentary: Birznieks I (2025). "C-tactile afferents: the mystery of human
> emotional touch has been hidden hair-deep." *Journal of Physiology*.

**What it found** *(verified).* 16 people, microneurography. Of 15 consecutive CT
units that had a visible hair in the receptive field, **all 15 responded to
deflection of that hair.** Firing during hair deflection averaged 12 spikes/s —
statistically indistinguishable from soft brushing. Optical coherence tomography
put the skin displacement from hair deflection at **8 µm**, against 17 µm from
the lightest monofilament used (0.4 mN). Six units kept firing after a hair was
plucked, for a mean of 7.1 s (SD 3.2).

**Weight.** New, small, single laboratory, not yet replicated elsewhere — and a
clean positive result on a specific mechanical question, consistent with what is
long established in mice.

**Where it would sit.** Cheapest as a **fifth correction card** in part four. A
richer option is to fold it into *found* (stop five), since it changes what the
1999 discovery was a discovery *of*.

**The figure.** The correction cards are the honest shape for it and cost almost
nothing. If it goes into *found* instead: a switch between deflecting a hair and
pressing the skin between hairs, with the CT firing rate beside it, and the two
displacements — 8 µm from a bent hair, 17 µm from the lightest filament — drawn
to the same scale. That comparison is the finding, and it is a nice one to see:
the more effective stimulus moved the skin less than half as far.

**What could go wrong.** "So it only works on hairy skin, and only through the
hairs" — which walks straight back into the claim Watkins 2021 retired and the
page already corrected. And *hairy skin* has to stay in plain language: the page
says *hairless* rather than *glabrous* for exactly this reason.

### 3.2 Peters, Hackeman & Goldreich 2009 — it is finger size, not sex

> Peters RM, Hackeman E, Goldreich D (2009). "Diminutive digits discern delicate
> details: fingertip size and the sex difference in tactile spatial acuity."
> *Journal of Neuroscience* 29(50):15756–15761.
> https://www.jneurosci.org/content/29/50/15756

**What it found** *(verified).* 100 recruited, 97 analysed (48 women, 49 men),
mean age about 20. Women resolved finer gratings, by **0.18 mm** (*t* = 1.79,
*p* = 0.038) — the same gap reported before. Fingertip surface area predicted the
threshold: *r* = 0.37 overall (*p* < 0.001), and it held **within** each sex
(women *r* = 0.30, men *r* = 0.36). In an ANCOVA with both terms, fingertip area
predicted acuity (*F*(1,94) = 11.7, *p* < 0.001) and **sex did not**
(*F* = 0.148, *p* = 0.65). Sweat pore density — a proxy for how tightly the
receptors are packed — was higher in smaller fingers (*r* = −0.50, *p* = 0.028,
*n* = 15).

**Weight.** Good sample for a psychophysics study, an effect that holds within
each sex as well as between them, and a proposed mechanism with independent
support. The size of the sex gap itself is small and its *p* is 0.038.

**Where it would sit.** *How fine* (stop three), beside the two-point figure.

**The figure.** The page's second predict-then-reveal, and a better one than the
first, because the reader has a wrong answer ready. Ask: *whose fingertips
resolve finer detail, women's or men's?* Almost everyone answers, most answer
"women's", and they are right — then the reveal takes the explanation away from
them. A fingertip-size slider drives a predicted threshold; two distributions
overlap heavily; controlling for size collapses the sex term to nothing.

It also gives the reader something about **their own hands**, which no figure on
the page currently does, and it is checkable — they can measure their own
fingertip in a minute.

**What could go wrong.** The finding is about *passive spatial acuity on the
fingertip*. It is not about being a better practitioner, and the page must not
let it become that; the relationship between resolving a grating and doing good
manual work is unmeasured. It is also a young adult sample, which matters given
§3.3.

### 3.3 Legge et al. 2008 and Stevens & Cruz 1996 — acuity, age, and use

> Legge GE, Madison C, Vaughn BN, Cheong AMY, Miller JC (2008). "Retention of
> high tactile acuity throughout the lifespan in blindness." *Perception &
> Psychophysics* 70(8):1471–1488. https://doi.org/10.3758/PP.70.8.1471
>
> Stevens JC, Cruz LA (1996). "Spatial acuity of touch: ubiquitous decline with
> aging revealed by repeated threshold testing." *Somatosensory & Motor
> Research* 13(1):1–10.

**What Legge found** *(verified).* 49 blind Braille readers aged 18–74 and 83
sighted people aged 12–82, tested on dot-pattern charts. Sighted acuity declined
**0.86% per year** — threshold spacing 1.46 mm at 20 rising to 2.43 mm at 80.
Blind participants showed **no significant decline** and sat around 1.20–1.26 mm
across the whole age range. Among the blind participants, acuity did **not**
correlate with Braille reading speed, daily reading amount, or the age at which
Braille was learned.

**Weight.** Good sizes for this literature, a large and consistent age effect,
and a cross-sectional design — so the blind–sighted difference is an association,
not a demonstration that use preserves acuity.

**Where it would sit.** *How fine* (stop three), as an age control on the
existing acuity figure.

**The figure.** An age slider on the two-point figure the page already has. Every
threshold grows as the reader drags from 20 to 80 — and this is the practical
point for a practitioner, whose clients are frequently at the far end of that
slider. A second track for the blind participants, flat across the same range,
turns it from a fact about decline into a question worth leaving open.

**What could go wrong.** "Use it or lose it" is the conclusion the figure invites
and the data do not support: cross-sectional, and within the blind group the
amount of Braille reading predicted nothing. If the second track is drawn, the
caption has to say that. This is a good candidate for the page's habit of ending
a stop with an open question rather than an instruction.

### 3.4 Hertenstein et al. 2009 — emotions read from touch alone

> Hertenstein MJ, Holmes R, McCullough M, Keltner D (2009). "The communication of
> emotion via touch." *Emotion* 9(4):566–573. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016108
>
> Earlier: Hertenstein MJ, Keltner D, App B, Bulleit BA, Jaskolka AR (2006).
> "Touch communicates distinct emotions." *Emotion* 6(3):528–533.

**What it found** *(verified from the abstract).* Strangers touched each other to
convey a named emotion, and the person touched chose from a list **without seeing
the touch**. Anger, fear, disgust, love, gratitude and sympathy were decoded
above chance, and so were happiness and sadness, which had not previously been
shown. Fine-grained coding recorded which touch behaviours went with which
emotion.

**Weight.** Replicated across two studies and two countries in the 2006 paper,
and a forced choice from a list — which measures whether an emotion can be told
apart from a fixed set of alternatives, not whether it would be recognised in a
room. **Per-emotion accuracies are not verified here — see §6.**

**Where it would sit.** *The organ* or a new stop in part one. It supports a
claim the page implies but never demonstrates: that touch is a channel with
content, not only a comfort.

**The figure.** A confusion matrix, which is the natural shape of the data and a
shape the page does not yet use. Tap an intended emotion, see where the reads
actually landed — including the wrong ones, which are the interesting part.
Pride, envy and embarrassment did not come through at all, and a matrix shows a
null as clearly as a hit.

**What could go wrong.** The leap from a decoding experiment to "your hands are
communicating whether you mean them to" — plausible, widely asserted, and not
what was measured. And the per-emotion numbers must come from the paper's own
table, not from any secondary account.

## 4. Tier three — real, with reservations that would have to be stated

**Nummenmaa et al. 2016** — "Social touch modulates endogenous μ-opioid system
activity in humans," *NeuroImage* 138:242–247. *(Verified.)* 18 men, two
[¹¹C]carfentanil PET scans; in one, their partner caressed them non-sexually.
Touch produced pleasurable sensations and increased μ-opioid receptor
availability in thalamus, striatum, and frontal, cingulate and insular cortices.
**Weight:** small, single-sex, and PET binding is an indirect measure whose
direction is genuinely argued over. **Why it is tempting:** it offers part four
something better supported than oxytocin. **Why to be careful:** "touch releases
endorphins" is precisely the kind of confident mechanism claim the page exists to
push back on, and n=18 does not license it. If it goes in, it goes in as *the
better-evidenced neighbour of a weak claim, still thin*, not as a replacement
mechanism.

**Schneider et al. 2023** — "Affectionate touch and diurnal oxytocin levels: an
ecological momentary assessment study," *eLife* 12:e81241. *(Verified.)* Survey
n=1,050; EMA n=247, six saliva samples a day over two days. Within-person,
momentary oxytocin was higher with **more intense** affectionate touch
(*b* = 0.006, *t*(149) = 3.058, *p* = 0.002); the mere **presence** of touch
showed no association. **Weight:** the best real-world test of the page's weakest
claim, and salivary oxytocin carries every measurement problem the authors
themselves discuss at length. **Where:** it refines the 0.26 in `CLAIMS` rather
than moving it, and gives the oxytocin stop something more useful than "the
evidence is thin" — namely *what a careful test looks like and what it found*.
It also fits the page's grain: intensity mattered, presence did not.

**Bolanowski et al. 1988** — "Four channels mediate the mechanical aspects of
touch," *J Acoust Soc Am* 84(5):1680–1694. *(Verified.)* Four psychophysical
channels — P, NP I, NP II, NP III — mapping onto PC, RA, SA II and SA I fibres,
together covering **0.4 Hz to beyond 500 Hz**, with overlapping sensitivities so
that any real stimulus drives several at once. **Weight:** foundational
psychophysics, still the standard framing, and a laboratory abstraction — the
channels are inferred from masking experiments, not observed. **The figure:** a
frequency dial on the existing receptor cross-section, lighting up whichever
receptors answer. It would make the anatomical figure functional, and it makes a
point the page's title is already making — that "touch" is several instruments
with different ranges. **The reservation:** it is a fifth thing to learn in a
stop that already introduces five receptor types, and HANDOFF §9 warns against
crowding the run-in to *meaning*.

**Tesarz et al. 2011** — "Sensory innervation of the thoracolumbar fascia in rats
and humans," *Neuroscience* 194:302–308. Quantitative in rat, preliminary and
non-quantitative in human. Dense sensory innervation of the thoracolumbar fascia,
markedly unequal across layers — subcutaneous tissue and outer layer densest.
The only ending type found was the free nerve ending; no corpuscular receptors.
Substance P-positive endings, presumed nociceptive, were confined to those outer
layers. **Weight:** mostly rat, human portion preliminary, and it is the honest
version of a claim that circulates in training rooms in a much larger form.
**Why it belongs anyway:** HANDOFF §5 records that the page's worst error was
caught by "a reader who works with tissue rather than skin," and the page still
has nothing for that reader. This says, carefully, that there is sensory supply
below the skin — and that what has actually been found there is not the rich
receptor array the skin has. That is a correction in both directions at once,
which is the page's characteristic move.

**Rossettini, Carlino & Testa 2018** — "Clinical relevance of contextual factors
as triggers of placebo and nocebo effects in musculoskeletal pain," *BMC
Musculoskeletal Disorders* 19:27. A taxonomy of contextual factors — internal
(the patient's memories, expectations), external (the treatment and the room),
relational (what the practitioner says and how). **Weight:** a narrative review
and a framework, not data, and it should never be cited as evidence of an effect
size. **Where:** it is the practitioner-facing bridge from Gazzola and Ellingsen
to the room, and it is written for exactly this audience.

**Mendell 2014** — "Constructing and deconstructing the gate theory of pain,"
*Pain* 155(2):210–216. What survived of the 1965 mechanism and what did not.
**Weight:** authoritative review by someone who worked on the original.
**Where:** the *pain* stop, in the `src` block. HANDOFF §4 already says "do not
present the 1965 mechanism as current detail" — this is the citation that lets
the page say *how* it was revised instead of only that it was.

## 5. Considered and not recommended

With the reason, so the judgement can be revisited rather than repeated.

**Skedung et al. 2013**, "Feeling small: exploring the tactile perception
limits," *Sci Rep* 3:2617 — fingers discriminate surface wrinkles down to about
10 nm amplitude. A genuinely astonishing number and a lovely zoom figure. Off the
spine: the page is about what touch does between people, and this is about
material discrimination. Best kept in reserve if part one ever needs a hook.

**The cutaneous rabbit** (Geldard & Sherrick 1972) — taps at the wrist and elbow
are felt hopping up the arm in between. Delightful, and it cannot be delivered
through a screen, so the page could only *assert* an illusion rather than produce
one. Every other interaction here either shows a mechanism or lets the reader
test it on themselves.

**Gordon et al. 2023**, *Nature* — the homunculus is interleaved with a
whole-body action network. A real correction to a picture most readers carry, but
it is motor cortex, and the page has no motor content to attach it to.

**Feldman et al. 2014** (kangaroo care at ten years) and **Johnston et al. 2017**
(Cochrane, skin-to-skin for procedural pain). Both strong, and both now sit
inside Packheiser's newborn stratum, which reaches the same conclusions with far
more studies. Adding development as a section is a length decision, not an
evidence one — as HANDOFF §7 already says.

**Manual therapy force variability** — measured forces differ substantially
between therapists (fair-to-poor inter-rater reliability, good intra-rater). A
tempting "you cannot calibrate your own hands" figure. The literature is small,
the protocols vary, and it points away from the page's subject toward technique.

**Tactile acuity in chronic pain** — a meta-analysis reports larger lumbar
two-point thresholds in chronic low back pain, with the deficit specific to the
painful region and inconsistent elsewhere. Contested, methodologically noisy, and
it risks the page being read as diagnostic — which HANDOFF §7 already flags for
clinical populations generally.

**von Mohr et al. 2021**, touch deprivation in COVID — intimate touch deprivation
tracked anxiety, but the association did not survive adjustment for loneliness.
Topical, self-report, cross-sectional. HANDOFF §7 already says it would need care;
that is still right.

## 6. Not verified — read before using

Do not put any of this on the page from this document.

**Ellingsen 2013 was cleared on 2026-09-06** and has moved to §2.4. Reading it
changed three things, which is the argument for this section existing — see the
corrections recorded there. Five items remain.

- **Hertenstein per-emotion accuracies.** Secondary accounts give "as high as
  78%", a 48–83% range, and a chance level of about 25%. None of that came from
  the paper. The 2006 and 2009 studies used different response sets, so the
  chance level differs between them. Read the tables.
- **Stevens & Cruz 1996** and **Stevens & Choo 1996** (body surface across the
  life span). The ~1% per year figure is consistently reported second-hand and
  the abstracts were not retrievable. Legge 2008's 0.86%/yr **is** verified and
  can stand alone if the others cannot be obtained.
- **Johnston et al. 2017** Cochrane numbers (PIPP mean differences at 30/60/90 s)
  came from a search summary. The review reported *I²* = 94% at 30 s, so any
  single pooled number needs its heterogeneity stated beside it.
- **Tesarz 2011 page range** (194:302–308) is from memory of the citation, not a
  metadata record. Confirm it.
- **Bolanowski channel frequency boundaries.** The 0.4–500+ Hz overall range is
  from the abstract. The per-channel crossover frequencies a figure would need
  are not — and they vary with contactor size and skin temperature, which is
  itself worth knowing before drawing anything.

`nameYY` convention already in use, given names as initials, diacritics in
author names as LaTeX escapes (`{\AA}`, `{\"o}`) while titles keep the
published characters as they stand, journal names spelled out in full, and
page ranges with an en-dash `--`.

**Checked, not just written.** Every entry below was run through the project's
own citeproc and `ama.csl` and read back. `{\AA}` gives Å, `{\"o}` gives ö,
`\&` gives &, `and others` gives *et al.*, and the raw μ, the `E1657--E1666`
range and `e81241` all survive.

**Do not add an entry until its prose lands.** `carry-back.qmd` pulls the whole
file in with `nocite: @*`, so a reference added ahead of the stop that cites it
appears in the closing list uncited — which is a content regression, and
`check.js` only notes it rather than failing.

across the six pages, because AMA numbers by order of appearance and the
